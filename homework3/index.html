Homework 3: Toy ECC Encryption, Caesar Cipher and Two Attacks

The same message goes through both schemes, then we break both of them. Everything runs in the browser.

1. Toy ECC encryption

This is the scheme from the previous homework turned into an actual cipher: the curve is still y² = x³ + 7 (the secp256k1 equation), but over a small 31-bit prime, and the private key is deliberately limited to a few bits. The construction is ElGamal / ECIES-like: instead of squeezing letters into curve points, the shared point seeds a keystream.

  1. Key generation. Bob picks a secret integer d (private key) and publishes Q = dG (public key).
  2. Encryption (Alice). She picks a fresh random r, computes C1 = rG and the shared secret S = rQ, turns S into a keystream and XORs it with the message.
  3. Decryption (Bob). He computes d·C1 = d·r·G = r·Q = S, rebuilds the same keystream and XORs again.

Roles of the keys: d never leaves Bob; Q and C1 are public; r is a one-time secret of Alice; S is the secret that only Alice (via r) and Bob (via d) can compute.

    3. Similarities and differences

    What they share: both are reversible transformations driven by a secret, and in both the decryption is the "undo" of the encryption. In the ECC scheme the heavy part is the public-key step that creates S; the actual message hiding is a stream cipher, just like Caesar is a (very weak) one with a constant keystream.
    

    
 
 
    

    


    4. Statistical cryptanalysis of Caesar
    

    Idea: for each of the 26 possible shifts, undo it and ask how much the result looks like English. "Looks like English" is measured against a reference corpus (first box). Three ways to score a candidate are compared: assuming the most frequent letter is E; χ² between the letter counts and the corpus frequencies; and the likelihood of the candidate under a bigram (letter pair) model.
    

    
 
 
 
    
 
    
 
    

    

    Effectiveness and limits. With a few hundred letters every method is practically always right, because the 26 candidates are very different in score. With 5–20 letters the sample is too small: letter counts are noisy, several shifts look equally plausible and the guess is often wrong. The E heuristic breaks first, since in a short text the most frequent letter is often not E. Bigrams help because a short text still contains many pairs; the exhaustive approach (print all 26 candidates and read them) is also reliable if a human is in the loop. All of this assumes the language is known, the text is natural prose and the corpus matches its style (a text without E, a list of names or another language would confuse the score).
    


    5. Attacking the toy ECC key
    

    The private key only has a few bits, so we can solve Q = dG directly from the public key. Two options: exhaustive search (compute G, 2G, 3G, … until one equals Q) and baby-step giant-step (store jG for j up to √N, then walk Q − i·mG until it hits the table: about √N steps instead of N, at the price of memory).
    

    
 
    Uses the key pair and the last ciphertext from section 1. Try 8, 12, 16 and 20 bits and watch the operation counts: exhaustive search doubles with every extra bit, BSGS grows only with the square root.
    
 
    
 
    

    


    6. Discussion
    

    Caesar attack vs ECC attack. Against Caesar we use the plaintext: we need to know what the language looks like and we only need the ciphertext. The test for a correct key is statistical ("this looks like English"), and it needs enough text to work. Against the toy ECC key we use only public data (G and Q) and the test for a correct key is exact: kG equals Q or it doesn't. It works for a one-word message as well as for a book, because the weakness is the number of possible keys, not the message.
    

    Why it doesn't scale. Exhaustive search costs about 2bits operations. For a real 256-bit key that is around 1077. Even the best generic algorithms (baby-step giant-step, Pollard's rho) cost about √(group order) ≈ 2128 steps, which is out of reach of any realistic computer, and we know no shortcut for well-chosen curves like secp256k1. Note also that the 20-bit BSGS test above is easy only because the exponent range is tiny.
    

    Weaknesses of the restricted key space. The tiny range of d makes brute force and BSGS trivial. Related: since the key is chosen in a very small range, any smarter search just needs the range, not the whole group.
    

    Weaknesses of the toy design itself (even with a big d).
    

      

    • The prime is only 31 bits, so the whole group has about 231 points and BSGS or Pollard's rho solves the discrete log in roughly 216 steps no matter how large d is.
      • 

    • The keystream is xorshift32 seeded with 32 bits of S: the effective secret is at most 232 and the generator is linear and invertible, so a known plaintext fragment reveals the whole stream.
      • 

    • No authentication: flipping a ciphertext bit flips the same plaintext bit, so messages can be tampered with.
      • 

    • If r were ever reused, XORing two ciphertexts gives m₁ ⊕ m₂ (the classic two-time pad).
