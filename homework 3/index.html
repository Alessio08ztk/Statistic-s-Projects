Homework 4: Penetration Scores, LLN and CLT

n systems, m attackers, and what happens to the aggregate score when n grows. Everything is simulated in the browser.

The model

Each attacker goes through the n systems one after the other, and Xi is the score obtained on the i-th one. The steps are independent and identically distributed. The m attackers are m independent copies of the whole trajectory, so a simulation with m attackers gives m samples of any quantity built from X1…Xn.

  • Bernoulli: Xi ∈ {0,1}, P(Xi=1)=p. A 1 means system i was penetrated. Mean μ = p, variance σ² = p(1−p).
  • Rademacher: Xi = ±1 with probability ½ each. Net score: +1 breach, −1 repelled, no drift. μ = 0, σ² = 1.

Three quantities per attacker: the total Sn, the average Sn/n and the normalised total Sn/√n.

4. What it shows

Variances. Var(Sn) = nσ² grows linearly. Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n goes to zero. Var(Sn/√n) = σ² does not depend on n: the √n is exactly the scaling that keeps the spread alive without blowing it up or killing it. The table reproduces the three behaviours.

Law of large numbers. Sn/n → μ in probability: for any ε the share of attackers whose average is farther than ε from μ goes to zero. Chebyshev gives the rate σ²/(nε²) as an upper bound; the simulated share is usually much smaller, so the bound is correct but not tight. In the Rademacher case the average goes to 0, in the Bernoulli case to p.

Central limit theorem. For the Rademacher model Sn/√n → N(0,1) in distribution. The histogram overlay gets closer to the bell and the KS distance shrinks with n (it never reaches exactly 0: Sn only takes values on a lattice, which costs on the order of 1/√n, and using a finite number of attackers adds noise of order 1/√m). For Bernoulli the same holds after centring: (Sn − np)/√n → N(0, p(1−p)); without centring Sn/√n drifts to infinity like p√n, which is what the third trajectory plot shows. The convergence is slower when p is close to 0 or 1 (the Bernoulli is very skewed there).

Systems and attackers. A larger system pool (n) makes the number of compromised systems larger and more variable in absolute terms (spread ∝ √n), but the fraction compromised is more predictable (spread ∝ 1/√n): big infrastructures behave almost deterministically on average, small ones are lucky or unlucky. More attackers (m) does not change the law of one attacker's score. It improves what we know about it: the sample mean over attackers has standard error σ√n/√m, and the histograms get smoother. If the attackers' scores are added up, the aggregate has mean mnμ and variance mnσ², so it follows the same laws with mn steps.

Caveats. Real systems are not independent (shared software, shared credentials) and attackers learn, so the i.i.d. assumption is optimistic: correlation would make the variance of the total grow faster than n and the average would concentrate more slowly.
