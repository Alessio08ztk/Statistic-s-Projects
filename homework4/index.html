Ex. 1 – Welford's recurrence formulas

Notation for a stream x1, x2, …: the mean after n values is x̄n = (1/n) Σ xi, the sum of squares is SSn = Σ (xi − x̄n)², and the sample variance is s²n = SSn/(n−1).

Knuth's recurrence for the mean (the model to imitate)

From n·x̄n = (n−1)·x̄n−1 + xn we get

x̄n = x̄n−1 + δn/n,   with δn = xn − x̄n−1

The new mean is the old mean plus a correction proportional to the surprise δn.

Recurrence for the sum of squares

Same idea: express SSn through SSn−1 and the new point. Start from the shortcut SSn = Σ xi² − n·x̄n². Subtracting the same formula for n−1:

SSn − SSn−1 = xn² − n·x̄n² + (n−1)·x̄n−1²

Write a = x̄n−1. Then xn = a + δ and x̄n = a + δ/n, so

xn² = a² + 2aδ + δ²
n·x̄n² = n·a² + 2aδ + δ²/n

Plug in; the a² and the 2aδ terms cancel:

SSn − SSn−1 = δ² − δ²/n = ((n−1)/n)·δ²

Since xn − x̄n = δ − δ/n = ((n−1)/n)·δ, the same quantity can be written as δ·(xn − x̄n), a product of the "old" and the "new" deviation. Therefore

SSn = SSn−1 + δn · (xn − x̄n),   SS1 = 0

Recurrence for the variance

With s²n = SSn/(n−1), i.e. SSn = (n−1)s²n, and SSn−1 = (n−2)s²n−1:

s²n = ((n−2)/(n−1))·s²n−1 + δn²/n,   n ≥ 2

For the "divide by n" variance σ̂²n = SSn/n the same computation gives n·σ̂²n = (n−1)·σ̂²n−1 + δn(xn − x̄n).

Code

Inside the loop, the core logic is three lines:

const d = x - mean; mean += d / n; ss += d * (x - mean);

Order matters: ss uses the old d and the already updated mean.

Why bother: numerical stability

The textbook one-pass formula (Σx² − (Σx)²/n)/(n−1) subtracts two huge, almost equal numbers when the data have a large mean and a small spread. Welford only ever works with small deviations.











Ex. 2 – Sampling mean and variance


This extends the penetration-score homework: each attempt now gets a score from 0 to 10 drawn from a fixed distribution with mean μ and variance σ². Conclusions


The averages match the theory. The average of the sample means is close to μ for every n, even for n = 2: the sample mean is an unbiased estimator, E[x̄] = μ. The average of s² with the n−1 divisor is close to σ² for every n: it is unbiased too. If we divide by n instead, the average sits at σ²(n−1)/n, visibly too low for small n and negligible for large n (the right-hand part of the fourth column). The reason is that deviations are measured from x̄, which is fitted on the same data, so they are a bit smaller than deviations from the true μ; the lost degree of freedom is exactly what n−1 repays.


Why this is useful in inferential statistics. μ and σ² are normally unknown; we only have one sample. This experiment shows that x̄ and s² are right on average, so using them as estimates does not push the answer in a systematic direction, and it also shows how far a single sample can be from the truth. That spread is what confidence intervals and tests are built on (the standard error of the mean is s/√n). Welford is how a program obtains them from a stream of data in one pass without storing it.


More systems per hacker. The variability of the estimates shrinks as n grows: the variance of the sample means follows σ²/n, and the variance of s² also decays like 1/n, though with a larger constant, because it depends on the fourth moment of the scores (a heavier-tailed or more skewed score distribution makes s² noisier). Both estimators are therefore consistent: single hackers' estimates concentrate around μ and σ². The histograms get narrower and the one for the means looks more and more like the normal curve (CLT, as in the previous homework). The number of hackers m does not change the quality of one estimate; it only makes the averages in the table, and the histograms, less noisy.
